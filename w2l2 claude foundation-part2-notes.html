core concepts

Original glossary table, text kept close to source 📋:

TermDefinitionSignificance
ConfidentialityThe principle that information should only be accessible to authorized individuals or systems, preventing unauthorized disclosure.It is a primary goal of cybersecurity, ensuring sensitive data remains private and protected from prying eyes.
IdentificationThe process of establishing a credible and unique identity for an individual or entity within a system.This is the foundational first step in securing any system, determining "who is who" before granting any access.
AuthenticationThe act of verifying a claimed identity, confirming that an individual or entity is indeed who they say they are.It acts as the gateway to a system, ensuring only legitimate users can proceed after their identity has been established.
AuthorizationThe process of defining and enforcing the specific access rights and privileges an authenticated individual or entity has within a system.This controls "what you can do" once inside a system, preventing authorized users from accessing unauthorized resources.
AccountabilityThe ability to trace actions within a system back to the responsible individual or entity, especially in the event of an incident.It ensures responsibility for security breaches can be assigned, facilitating incident response and preventing recurrence.
VulnerabilityA weakness or flaw in a system, design, or implementation that could be exploited to compromise security.These are the entry points for attackers, and identifying and mitigating them is crucial for proactive security.
ExploitA piece of software, data, or sequence of commands that takes advantage of a vulnerability to cause unintended behavior.This is the active mechanism used by attackers to leverage weaknesses and gain unauthorized access or control.
False PositiveAn incorrect detection by a security system, where legitimate activity or data is mistakenly flagged as malicious.While not as dangerous as false negatives, frequent false positives can lead to security fatigue and systems being disabled.
Trade-offThe inherent conflict between achieving high levels of security and maintaining operational efficiency or user convenience.Balancing security measures with practical operational needs is a constant challenge in cybersecurity management.

01 · detailed analysis

the foundational trio: identification → authentication → authorization

This is a sequential, interdependent chain 🔗 — and the whole basis of confidentiality:

🪪
identification
"who is who?" e.g. Aadhaar, an IIT enrollment
➡️
👆
authentication
prove it! ID card at the gate, fingerprint match
➡️
🚦
authorization
what can you actually do now?
📸 a fingerprint scanner — the classic "something you have/are" authentication method

🪪 identification answers "who is who?" — think Aadhaar's biometric enrollment, or getting your student ID at IIT Madras. No identity = no way to grant (or deny) access.

👆 authentication proves the claim is real — flashing your ID card at the gate, or matching a fingerprint against stored Aadhaar data. Two flavors: something you have (ID card, biometric) and something you know (password, PIN). Logging into Gmail = user ID (identification) + password (authentication). Highly sensitive systems add multi-factor authentication (MFA) 🔐.

🚦 authorization decides what you're allowed to do after you're in. A student can enter their hostel room, not a faculty office 🚪. On Moodle, student and faculty logins get totally different privilege levels. Good authorization needs clear classification of info + roles, then precise access mapping.

02 · detailed analysis

accountability: responsibility & learning from incidents

📸 the quiet enforcer of accountability — logs and cameras that answer "who did this?"

Accountability answers: "who is responsible?" when things break. Unauthorized entry into a secure campus area? Time to review security personnel and their actions 🕵️.

In cyber terms, it means tracing exactly who accessed what, and how — via comprehensive logs: sign-in/out times, access attempts, resource usage. No logs = no forensic trail = no way to actually fix the root cause. 📋

Skip logging, and you lose the ability to learn from a breach — and to prove who's responsible.
03 · case study

the Target data breach (2013)

📸 Target — hit during the peak 2013 holiday season, in one of retail's most-studied breaches

A textbook case of tech failure + management failure combining into disaster 💥 — right during Thanksgiving/Christmas shopping season.

🚪 the entry point

Attackers got in through Faisal Mechanical Services, a third-party HVAC/climate-control vendor with network access to Target's systems.

💳 unencrypted POS data

Card info sat unencrypted in POS terminal RAM — transmission to central servers was encrypted, but the capture point wasn't.

🔕 malware detection: off

Target's malware detection software had been switched off — the system meant to catch exactly this kind of attack.

🔓 authorization failure

A vendor limited to climate control was granted access to payment systems — privileges far beyond their actual job.

🚨 too many false positives

Detection got disabled partly due to alarm fatigue from frequent false positives — efficiency won over security.

📉 incomplete logging

Logging gaps made accountability and incident response even harder after the fact.

📸 a POS card terminal — the exact device type at the center of the breach
Result: millions of stolen credit card & personal records, major financial loss, reputational damage, and legal fallout for Target.

04 · detailed analysis

vulnerabilities & exploits: the attacker's advantage

vulnerability

a weakness ready to be leveraged — like an unlocked classroom door.

exploit

knowing the door's unlocked and walking through it to cause trouble.

Not every vulnerability gets exploited — but Target's unencrypted data + disabled detection + excessive vendor access was a perfect storm that did. ⛈️

False positives (legit activity flagged as malicious) are annoying but far less dangerous than false negatives (a real threat slipping through unnoticed) — the real danger is when too many false positives push teams to switch detection off entirely. 🔕

🌟 revision time

key takeaways

  • identification is step one — establishing who someone is.
  • authentication verifies that claim — "something you know" or "something you have".
  • authorization sets access rights — stopping authenticated users from going where they shouldn't.
  • IAA are sequential & interdependent — together they form confidentiality's backbone.
  • accountability = assigning responsibility, learning from incidents.
  • logging is essential for forensic analysis & accountability.
  • breaches = tech flaws + managerial oversight, almost always together.
  • the security vs. efficiency trade-off is a constant management tension.
  • disabling protections over false positives = a critical, avoidable vulnerability.
  • third-party vendor access needs to be tightly scoped, always.
🏁 wrap up

conclusion

Cybersecurity is never just technical — it's a mix of technical safeguards, human behavior, and organizational policy. Identification, authentication and authorization give systems their structural integrity 🏗️.

But as Target proved, even solid tech defenses crumble under bad management calls — disabling detection, over-granting vendor access. The tension between efficiency and security never fully goes away. Real cybersecurity needs a culture of accountability, constant vigilance, and a sharp eye for exploitable gaps. 👁️
